People.Place.Purpose.

A community-powered rating system for data centers

This draft starts from the community. A data center earns its place when local people share in what it creates, when it is clean, and when the work it does helps them. We score all three, the way LEED scores buildings, so neighbors, councils and engineers can read the same scorecard.

Rebecca Butler and Christina Liebner · Draft v0.1 · November 2026 · For discussion

Community first

How do we build and operate a data center that aligns with our community's needs and values?

Most stories about data centers start from fear: bills that rise, wells that run low, jobs that go elsewhere, decisions made far away. Those fears are reasonable. A data center is where they become concrete. It has an address, a zoning hearing and a fence.

We started from that question instead. Residents would want a fair share of what a site earns, a real voice in what happens next, clean power and water they can trust, and work that helps the people who live there. Each of those can be measured. We were inspired by existing community tools and rating systems, from the NAACP's community benefits agreement template to LEED, and brought them together into one rubric that starts from the community.

LEED gave buildings a shared language for "green." Common Ground Compute aims to do the same for data centers: a public scorecard that a council member, a utility engineer and a neighbor at the farmers market can all read. Our bias is toward small, clean, community-owned sites, and large projects can earn their way up with verified commitments.

How communities can use this

A discussion guide first, a scorecard second

Use the three pillars to decide what your community values before a developer calls. Then use the scorecard to hold any proposal, and any site that gets built, to what you wrote down.

  1. 1

    Before a developer calls

    Talk through the pillar questions at a library, council or neighborhood meeting. Leave with your priorities, minimum asks and deal-breakers.

  2. 2

    When a proposal arrives

    Turn those priorities into specific asks, score the proposal against them, and write the commitments into an enforceable agreement.

  3. 3

    After it is built

    Read the site's annual public report, re-rate it every three years, and ask residents whether it has been good for the community.

Where the circles meet

Each overlap is a promise a community can check

Fair footprint

People and Place. Clean, quiet, flexible on the grid, and paying its own way, so neighbors are not stuck with the costs.

Shared prosperity

People and Purpose. Local ownership, local services and local data, so the value and the decisions stay close to home.

Clean, useful compute

Place and Purpose. Energy and water go into work that matters, measured as carbon and water per unit of useful work.

Common ground

All three. Ten center points reward integrated design, an open playbook, and an independent survey of what residents actually think.

What each pillar asks for

The site, as it operates

The rating covers one site: the facility, its owner and operator, and the work it hosts. A green building with poor data practices scores well in Place and poorly in Purpose, and the balance rule keeps it out of the top tiers.

Plain-language intent first, then the measurable requirement. Thresholds are a conservative first draft for testing, not final values.

Place

Is it clean, efficient, and easy on local water and the grid?
30points

How the site is built and powered: clean energy hour by hour, efficiency judged fairly for the climate, water, grid flexibility and materials.

IDCreditRequirementPoints
Prerequisites
PL-P1
Metering and public disclosure
You can't manage or trust what isn't measured.
Revenue-grade sub-metering of IT and facility energy at 15-minute or finer intervals, and water at monthly or finer. Annual public report of energy, water, PUE, WUE and emissions.
Measured as: kWh, m3, PUE, WUE, tCO2e
Required
PL-P2
No fossil prime power
The site does not burn fuel to run day to day.
No on-site fossil generation for normal operation. Backup generation limited to emergencies, utility-requested grid support and testing, with run hours and emissions disclosed annually.
Measured as: Generator run hours, fuel use, emissions
Required
Clean power
PL1
Hourly clean energy matching
Power comes from clean sources in the same hours the site uses it.
2 pts: 100% annual matching with clean energy from the same grid region. 4 pts: at least 70% hourly carbon-free energy (CFE). 6 pts: at least 85%. 8 pts: at least 95%.
Measured as: Hourly CFE % = clean MWh matched in the same hour / load MWh
8
PL2
New clean supply and storage
Adds new clean power and storage instead of using up what already exists.
1 pt: contracts for new clean generation in the same grid region covering at least 50% of annual load. 2 pts: at least 100%. +1 pt: on-site or adjacent storage of at least 2 hours at 25% of peak IT load.
Measured as: MW and MWh contracted, commercial operation dates, storage MW and MWh
3
Efficiency
PL3
Climate-adjusted energy efficiency
Little energy is wasted on cooling and power conversion, judged fairly for the local climate.
Annual measured PUE compared with a climate-zone baseline (baselines to be set). 2 pts: at baseline. 3 pts: overhead (PUE minus 1) at least 15% below baseline. 4 pts: at least 30% below.
Measured as: Annual PUE from 12 months of measured data
4
PL4
Useful work and utilization
Equipment is actually used, and the site reports energy and carbon per unit of useful work.
1 pt: publish energy and carbon per unit of useful work by workload class (method to be defined). 1 pt: annual average server or accelerator utilization of at least 40%.
Measured as: kWh and gCO2e per unit of work; utilization %
2
PL5
Heat reuse
Waste heat warms something useful nearby.
1 pt: Energy Reuse Factor (ERF) of at least 0.10. 2 pts: at least 0.30.
Measured as: ERF = reused energy / total facility energy
2
Water
PL6
Water use efficiency
Uses little water, especially where water is scarce.
1 pt: site WUE of 0.5 L/kWh or less. 2 pts: 0.2 or less. 3 pts: 0.05 or less, or closed-loop or dry cooling. In high water-stress basins, 0.2 or less is required to earn any points.
Measured as: WUE in litres per kWh of IT energy; basin water stress
3
PL7
Non-potable water
Does not compete with drinking water.
1 pt: at least 90% of cooling water is recycled, reclaimed or otherwise non-potable, or no water is used for cooling.
Measured as: Share of cooling water by source
1
Grid citizenship
PL8
Flexible load
Can step back when the grid is stressed or dirty.
Verified ability to reduce demand on a utility or grid-operator signal for at least 4 hours. 1 pt: at least 10% of peak demand. 2 pts: at least 25%. 3 pts: at least 40%, or a flexible (curtailable) interconnection agreement. Tested annually.
Measured as: kW reduced, duration, event performance
3
PL9
Grid support and resilience
Helps keep the lights on for others.
1 pt: contracted grid services (such as frequency, voltage or capacity) from on-site storage. 1 pt: islanding capability that can serve designated community critical loads or a public resilience hub during outages, with an agreement in place.
Measured as: Contracted MW; critical load served, hours
2
Materials and lifecycle
PL10
Embodied carbon and hardware lifecycle
A lower-carbon building, and old equipment reused or properly recycled.
1 pt: whole-building life-cycle assessment showing at least 20% lower embodied carbon than a baseline design. 1 pt: at least 90% by weight of retired IT equipment reused or recycled through certified recyclers.
Measured as: kgCO2e per m2 or per kW; % of retired equipment by weight

How the rating works

100 points, four tiers, and a balance rule

9
Prerequisites

Disclosure and basic protections. Miss one and the project is not eligible, whatever its points.

30·30·30
Three pillars, equal weight

People, Place and Purpose each carry 30 points. Ten more sit in the center, for work that ties all three together.

≥12
Balance rule

Higher tiers need a minimum score in every pillar. A project cannot reach the Include tier on clean power alone.

Design principles

Conservative by design

  1. Measured, not modeled.Final ratings use 12 months of operating data. Ratings at the design stage are provisional.
  2. Hourly where it matters.Clean energy and flexibility are counted hour by hour, because annual totals hide the nights and still days.
  3. Balance is required.Minimum scores in every pillar keep a project from buying its way up with one strength.
  4. Disclosure before points.Prerequisites cover transparency and basic protections. Fail one and nothing else counts.
  5. Built on existing standards.ISO/IEC 30134 for PUE, WUE and heat reuse, the GHG Protocol for emissions, and established security and recycling certifications. See what we built on.
  6. Fair to small and large sites.Credits use shares and ratios, so a library-sized site and a large campus are judged on the same terms.
  7. Ratings expire.Sites recertify every three years with fresh data, and points can be lost.
  8. Climate-fair efficiency.Efficiency is judged against a climate-zone baseline, and carbon and water per unit of useful work lead, so sites can be built where people live.

What we built on

Community tools, rating systems, standards and research behind this draft

Community tools we learned from

  • NAACP community benefits agreement templateFrom the Stop Dirty Data Centers campaign. Enforceable commitments a community can negotiate.
  • Before Breaking GroundFederation of American Scientists. A guide for local governments on ordinances, agreements and tax effects, and on deciding priorities before negotiating.
  • Data Center Community Impact ScorecardUnautomated. Fifty questions that sharpen a community's requirements at the permit and design stage. We add a rating based on measured data that repeats over the life of the site.
  • Community benefits agreements for data centersWorld Resources Institute. Guidance and a database of community benefit frameworks.
  • New York Community Investment FrameworkA voluntary benchmark of $1 million in community investment per megawatt.
  • Thrive MetricsBlueprint Collaborative. A community-defined way to measure whether investment delivers, built on three rights: to live, to stay and belong, and to thrive.
  • IAP2 Spectrum of Public ParticipationInternational Association for Public Participation. Our tier names follow its levels of public influence.

Rating systems we learned from

  • LEEDPoints, prerequisites and tiers for green buildings. The model for our scorecard.
  • Green GlobesGreen Building Initiative. Sustainability certification that includes data center campuses.
  • B Corp certificationVerified social and environmental performance for whole companies, not just buildings.
  • The Green GridThe industry group that introduced PUE, the most common data center efficiency metric.

About us

Common Ground Compute is an open framework that helps a community decide which data centers to welcome and which to turn away.

We're Rebecca Butler and Christina Liebner, two engineers with nearly 40 years combined in clean energy, the power grid and data center efficiency. We're hopeful about the future of AI. It can help people start and run businesses, learn new skills at their own pace, spend more time with the people they love, and rapidly advance science and medicine. We've seen firsthand how useful it can be at work, and we used it to explore ideas and draft this framework.

That future depends on who owns AI and who benefits from it. We want one where power is distributed and AI is owned by all of us. So far, fear has driven most of the public conversation, and most of the benefits have gone to a few large companies.

We have concerns too. We worry about what AI means for people's jobs and families. Tech companies have let their climate goals slip in the rush to build. New data centers are increasingly off-grid, fossil-powered campuses, planned with little public notice. Communities are pushing back, especially when the answers on power, water, cost and benefit are vague. We got tired of feeling powerless, so we built a rating system.

It scores a site on three pillars: Place, People and Purpose. The draft is open source, and we're taking comments.

We're looking for collaborators to help make the framework better. Get in touch or write to hello@commongroundcompute.org.
